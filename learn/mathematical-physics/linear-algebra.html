Mathematical Methods · Linear Algebra

Introduction to
Linear Algebra

The language of quantum mechanics, electrodynamics, and all of modern physics.

Linear algebra is arguably the single most important mathematical tool in theoretical physics. Every quantum state is a vector. Every observable is a linear operator. Every symmetry is a group acting linearly on a space. Before you can seriously tackle quantum mechanics, you must be fluent in this language.

Why does it matter for physics?

In quantum mechanics, states live in a Hilbert space — an infinite-dimensional vector space with an inner product. The eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian operator are the energy levels of the system. In classical mechanics, small oscillations around equilibrium are governed by a matrix eigenvalue problem. In electrodynamics, the polarisation states of light form a 2D vector space. The pattern repeats everywhere.

💡 The central idea: Linear algebra studies linear maps between vector spaces — maps that respect addition and scalar multiplication. Almost all of physics is built on linearity (with small nonlinear corrections).

Chapter 2

Vector Spaces

The abstract setting where all of linear algebra lives.

Definition — Vector Space
A vector space \(V\) over a field \(\mathbb{F}\) (usually \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{C}\)) is a set with two operations — vector addition and scalar multiplication — satisfying 8 axioms: closure, associativity, commutativity, identity element \(\mathbf{0}\), inverse \(-\mathbf{v}\), scalar distributivity, vector distributivity, and compatibility.

Key concepts

Linear Independence

A set of vectors \(\{\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_n\}\) is linearly independent if the only solution to

Definition
\[ c_1 \mathbf{v}_1 + c_2 \mathbf{v}_2 + \cdots + c_n \mathbf{v}_n = \mathbf{0} \] is \(c_1 = c_2 = \cdots = c_n = 0\). Otherwise the set is linearly dependent.

Basis and Dimension

A basis of \(V\) is a linearly independent set that spans \(V\) — every vector in \(V\) can be written as a unique linear combination of basis vectors. The number of basis vectors is the dimension \(\dim V\).

⚛️ Physics link: In quantum mechanics, a complete set of orthonormal eigenstates \(\{|n\rangle\}\) of the Hamiltonian forms a basis for the Hilbert space. The superposition principle is just the statement that any state \(|\psi\rangle = \sum_n c_n |n\rangle\) is a linear combination.

Subspace

A subset \(W \subseteq V\) is a subspace if it is itself a vector space (closed under addition and scalar multiplication, and contains \(\mathbf{0}\)). The set of all solutions to a homogeneous linear system \(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\) is always a subspace — it's called the null space of \(A\).

✏️ Example — \(\mathbb{R}^3\)

The standard basis of \(\mathbb{R}^3\) is \(\{\hat{e}_1, \hat{e}_2, \hat{e}_3\}\) where

\[\hat{e}_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix},\quad \hat{e}_2 = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix},\quad \hat{e}_3 = \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}\]

These are linearly independent and span all of \(\mathbb{R}^3\), so \(\dim\mathbb{R}^3 = 3\). Any vector \(\mathbf{r} = x\hat{e}_1 + y\hat{e}_2 + z\hat{e}_3\).

Chapter 3

Linear Transformations

Matrices are representations of linear maps between vector spaces.

Definition — Linear Map
A map \(T: V \to W\) is linear if for all \(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in V\) and \(\alpha\in\mathbb{F}\): \[T(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}) = T\mathbf{u} + T\mathbf{v} \qquad \text{and} \qquad T(\alpha\mathbf{v}) = \alpha T\mathbf{v}\]

Matrix Representation

Once you choose bases for \(V\) and \(W\), every linear map \(T: V\to W\) can be represented as a matrix \([T]\). The columns of \([T]\) are the images of the basis vectors of \(V\), expressed in the basis of \(W\).

Kernel and Image

Key subspaces
\[\ker T = \{\mathbf{v}\in V : T\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\} \qquad \text{(null space)}\] \[\operatorname{im} T = \{T\mathbf{v} : \mathbf{v}\in V\} \qquad \text{(column space)}\]

Rank-Nullity Theorem

One of the most important results in linear algebra:

Rank-Nullity Theorem
\[\dim(\ker T) + \dim(\operatorname{im} T) = \dim V\] \[\text{nullity}(A) + \text{rank}(A) = n \quad \text{(for an } m\times n \text{ matrix)}\]
⚛️ Physics link: In quantum mechanics, the kernel of \((\hat{H} - E\,\hat{I})\) is the eigenspace of energy \(E\). Its dimension is the degeneracy of that energy level.

Change of Basis

If \(P\) is the change-of-basis matrix from old basis to new basis, then a linear operator \(A\) transforms as:

Similarity transformation
\[A' = P^{-1} A P\]

Two matrices related by a similarity transformation represent the same linear map in different bases. They share the same eigenvalues, trace, and determinant.

Chapter 4

Eigenvalues &
Eigenvectors

The most important computation in all of theoretical physics.

Definition — Eigenvector
A non-zero vector \(\mathbf{v}\) is an eigenvector of \(A\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda\) if \[A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}\]

The Characteristic Equation

Rearranging: \((A - \lambda I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\). For non-trivial solutions, the matrix \((A-\lambda I)\) must be singular:

Characteristic equation
\[\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\] This is an \(n\)-th degree polynomial in \(\lambda\) — the characteristic polynomial of \(A\). Its roots are the eigenvalues.

Trace and Determinant Relations

Key identities
\[\operatorname{tr}(A) = \sum_i \lambda_i \qquad \det(A) = \prod_i \lambda_i\]

Physical Meaning

⚛️ Quantum mechanics: The eigenvalue equation \(\hat{H}|\psi\rangle = E|\psi\rangle\) is the time-independent Schrödinger equation. The eigenvalues \(E\) are the allowed energy levels. The eigenvectors \(|\psi\rangle\) are the stationary states.
✏️ Example — \(2\times 2\) matrix

Find the eigenvalues of \(A = \begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}\).

\[\det\begin{pmatrix}3-\lambda&1\\1&3-\lambda\end{pmatrix}=(3-\lambda)^2-1=0\] \[(3-\lambda)^2=1 \implies \lambda = 2 \text{ or } \lambda = 4\]

Eigenvectors: for \(\lambda=4\), \(\mathbf{v} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,1)^T\); for \(\lambda=2\), \(\mathbf{v} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,-1)^T\).

Algebraic vs Geometric Multiplicity

The algebraic multiplicity of \(\lambda\) is its multiplicity as a root of \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\). The geometric multiplicity is \(\dim\ker(A-\lambda I)\) — the number of linearly independent eigenvectors. A matrix is diagonalisable if and only if the geometric multiplicity equals the algebraic multiplicity for every eigenvalue.

Chapter 5

Inner Products &
Orthogonality

Geometry, length, and angles — in any vector space.

Definition — Inner Product
An inner product \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle: V\times V \to \mathbb{C}\) satisfies: conjugate symmetry \(\langle u,v\rangle = \overline{\langle v,u\rangle}\), linearity in second argument, and positive definiteness \(\langle v,v\rangle \geq 0\) with equality iff \(v=0\). A vector space with an inner product is an inner product space (if complete: a Hilbert space).

Orthogonality and Norm

Norm and orthogonality
\[\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}\rangle}\] \[\mathbf{u}\perp\mathbf{v} \iff \langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle = 0\]

Gram-Schmidt Process

Given any basis \(\{\mathbf{v}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{v}_n\}\), Gram-Schmidt produces an orthonormal basis \(\{\mathbf{e}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{e}_n\}\):

Gram-Schmidt
\[\mathbf{u}_k = \mathbf{v}_k - \sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{\langle\mathbf{v}_k,\mathbf{u}_j\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{u}_j,\mathbf{u}_j\rangle}\,\mathbf{u}_j, \qquad \mathbf{e}_k = \frac{\mathbf{u}_k}{\|\mathbf{u}_k\|}\]

Projection

The orthogonal projection of \(\mathbf{v}\) onto a subspace \(W\) with orthonormal basis \(\{\mathbf{e}_i\}\):

Projection formula
\[\text{proj}_W\mathbf{v} = \sum_i \langle\mathbf{e}_i,\mathbf{v}\rangle\,\mathbf{e}_i\]
⚛️ Dirac notation: In quantum mechanics \(\langle\phi|\psi\rangle\) is the inner product. The probability amplitude of measuring state \(|n\rangle\) given system in state \(|\psi\rangle\) is \(\langle n|\psi\rangle\). The completeness relation \(\sum_n |n\rangle\langle n| = \hat{I}\) is just the projection formula written in Dirac notation.

Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality

Cauchy-Schwarz
\[|\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle|^2 \leq \langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}\rangle\,\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}\rangle\]

In quantum mechanics, combined with the Robertson uncertainty relation, this is the mathematical origin of the Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle.

Chapter 6

Diagonalization &
Spectral Theorem

The crown jewel — and the mathematical heart of quantum mechanics.

Diagonalization

An \(n\times n\) matrix \(A\) is diagonalisable if there exists an invertible matrix \(P\) such that:

Diagonalization
\[A = P\,D\,P^{-1}\] where \(D = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)\) and the columns of \(P\) are the eigenvectors of \(A\).

This is useful because \(A^n = P\,D^n\,P^{-1}\) and \(e^A = P\,e^D\,P^{-1}\), making matrix powers and functions easy to compute.

Hermitian Operators

Hermitian (self-adjoint) operator
An operator \(A\) is Hermitian if \(A = A^\dagger\), where \(A^\dagger = \overline{A^T}\) (conjugate transpose). In Dirac notation: \(\langle\phi|A|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|A|\phi\rangle^*\).

Properties of Hermitian operators

⚛️ 1. All eigenvalues of a Hermitian operator are real.
2. Eigenvectors belonging to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.
3. The eigenvectors form a complete orthonormal basis — this is the Spectral Theorem.

The Spectral Theorem

Spectral decomposition
\[A = \sum_i \lambda_i\,|\mathbf{e}_i\rangle\langle\mathbf{e}_i| = \sum_i \lambda_i\,P_i\] where \(P_i = |\mathbf{e}_i\rangle\langle\mathbf{e}_i|\) are orthogonal projection operators, \(P_i^2=P_i\), \(P_i P_j = \delta_{ij}P_i\), and \(\sum_i P_i = I\).

Unitary Matrices

A matrix \(U\) is unitary if \(U^\dagger U = UU^\dagger = I\), i.e. \(U^{-1}=U^\dagger\). Unitary matrices preserve inner products and have eigenvalues of unit modulus \(|\lambda|=1\). Time evolution in quantum mechanics, \(|\psi(t)\rangle = e^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar}|\psi(0)\rangle\), is generated by a unitary operator because \(\hat{H}\) is Hermitian.

Unitary diagonalization of Hermitian operators
\[A = U\,D\,U^\dagger, \qquad D = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\] where \(U\) is unitary and the \(\lambda_i\) are real eigenvalues.
